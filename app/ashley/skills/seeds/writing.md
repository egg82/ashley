# Method for writing work

The aim is text a reader takes at face value: clear, specific, and in the person's own voice, not one that sounds
produced. Readers who read a lot of machine text spot it within a few hundred words, and once they have, they stop
trusting the writer.

## Start

1. **Name the kind of text, its reader and what the reader should do or know afterwards.** An apology, a tutorial, a
   product label, a speech and a chapter of fiction each follow different rules. If you cannot name the reader, ask.
2. **Find the person's voice before using yours.** If the bench holds their earlier writing, a style note or a
   document they like, read it first and write toward it. Their decisions about voice beat any rule here.
3. **The points come from the person.** Ask what they want said. Use your skill for phrasing and structure, not to
   supply opinions, anecdotes or experiences. Never invent a story, a quote, a number or a first-hand moment in text
   that goes out under someone's name. If a detail is missing, ask for it or leave a visible gap.

## Draft

- **Say it plainly first.** Write the paragraph in the plainest words that carry the meaning. If the plain version
  says nothing, it needs a point before it needs better words.
- **Concrete beats general.** One particular object, number, name or act does more than an adjective.
- **People do things.** Make the person or thing the sentence is about the subject and the action the verb. "The
  committee intends to improve morale", not "the intention of the committee is the improvement of morale".
- **End where the thought ends.** Skip the tail clause that restates the sentence, the closing line that sums up the
  paragraph, and the neat moral. End on the specific thing or on the actual request.
- **Inform or leave a gap, by kind.** In fiction and lore, leave the last step to the reader and end on something
  concrete. Everywhere else (instructions, explanations, messages, anything with an ask) state the point outright;
  a reader left to infer can infer wrongly.
- **Names come from outside you.** Titles, product names and character names you propose cluster on the same few.
  Offer options for the person to choose from, or build names by a rule they set.

## Revise, in this order

Fixing a sentence the next step would cut is wasted work.

1. **Substance.** Check every number, name, quote and claim against something you were given or looked up, or cut it.
   For each claim ask "so what?" and "how do we know?".
2. **Words.** Replace buzzwords and category nouns with the thing and its number or name ("drive results", "robust
   solutions"). Use the short everyday word. Cut hedges that only soften; keep a hedge that says how sure anyone is,
   especially in medical, legal and safety text.
3. **Sentences.** Find the people and the actions, put them in subject and verb. Keep a passive when the known thing
   should open the sentence or the actor is unknown.
4. **Rhythm.** Mix lengths. Read it aloud. Count dashes, lists of three and closing summaries; when they repeat,
   restructure rather than swapping punctuation.
5. **Cold read.** Read as someone who has none of your context. What would they ask?

## Habits that mark machine text

Look for these in your own draft, and fix the thought under each, not just the wording:

- Stock praise and filler openings ("great question", "certainly", a sentence that announces what is coming).
- The same upbeat shape in every message: acknowledgement, point, offer of more help.
- Pairs of near-synonyms, lists of exactly three, "not just X but Y", a dramatic short line after a long one.
- Vague intensifiers and abstract nouns where a number or a name would go.
- A moral or summary in the last line.
- Over-even grammar: every sentence about the same length, no fragments, no passives, no contractions where a person
  would use them.

A list of banned words does not work: swapping words leaves the vague sentence in place. Fresh examples of real human
writing of the same kind move your output more than a rule does. Ask the person for one, and keep their sentences when
you edit their text.

## Short text and live text

- **Interface text and messages:** say what happened, what it means for the reader and what to do next, in that order,
  in the words the person would use for it. An error says what to fix. A label names the thing.
- **Sameness shows across texts, not inside one.** When you write many lines (a set of descriptions, a batch of
  replies), read them side by side and change any repeated shape.
- **Other languages:** do not translate English habits. Politeness, address and punctuation differ; ask for a native
  example when the person has one.

## Handing it back

Give the text, then at most a line on what you assumed and what you could not check. Name any gap left for the person to
fill, set apart where they will see it.
